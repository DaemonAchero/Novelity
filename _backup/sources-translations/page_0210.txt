In the heavy rain a burst of dense screams suddenly rose, and quickly the muffled grunts of bodies hitting the ground. "You!?" The rough voice seemed to want to say something, but before it could open its mouth it stopped short. Before long, out of the curtain of rain, Wei He came back, several small leather pouches in his hands, and some rain-cloaks besides.
He threw the cloaks down on the ground; there was still a hint of blood-smell on him. He grinned at the three of them. "I was just worrying where to find you some rain gear — and here people come and deliver it to my door."
"You… you killed them all!?" Guan Ye could not hold the question in. She ran a great business, and she had seen enough of scheming and killing, in both directions, but a man who could talk of killing as easily as Wei He — that was a first for her.
== Chapter 70 · Departure (Part 2) ==
"I went back and told them not to come in, but they wouldn't listen." Wei He said flatly. "Out here in the wilds, you don't really think they came to shelter from the rain? What are the odds of that? We arrive, and they arrive?" "And besides." He smiled. "When I went out there were just two men with knives, meaning to come in by the window and cut us down. Old hands, from the look of them." Guan Ye stared at him blankly, and suddenly felt that this was the first time she had met the real him.
This young man, plainly not much to look at, at this moment seemed to her of better style than her own big brother. "Light a fire first, then eat something; when the rain stops we move on." Wei He said no more, and crouched down to start the fire. As for firewood, one sweep of his eyes and he smashed one of the wooden god-statues down with a single palm, split it into strips with his bare hands and stacked them into a fire, then lit it with tinder.
The shrine's statues were interesting too: the god enshrined here was not one of the Three Pure Ones, but Taishi. Taishi Yuanjun was the highest god of the Daoism of these parts, and a statue that any temple, great or small, would enshrine no matter what. The four sat around together, and Guan Ye kept asking Wei He things: why he hid his strength, why he always needed poisons, whether he really was eighteen this year and not twenty-eight… why he so often would not talk, and
whether there was any woman he liked…… "A pity…… just now, I nearly fell for you." Guan Ye said, chewing a dripping roast meat cake, her words a little thick in her mouth. "Rest easy, you're not my type either." Wei He said absently, poking at the fire. "By the way, Brother Guan, do you have a way of telling whether strange-beast meat is poisonous?" The thought had come to him, and he asked it suddenly.
"That's simple." Guan Ye smiled, and began to explain in careful detail which methods would quickly identify whether strange-beast meat contained poison. Because strange-beast meat had a vitality unlike ordinary flesh, it was extremely hard for ordinary poisons to work their way in and still keep their original form. So a careful look at the meat's appearance and shape was enough to tell whether poison had been put inside.
Wei He listened closely, from time to time pulling out a charcoal pencil and a slip of leather paper to take notes. The rain fell the whole night through. In the time he spent escorting the three Guan family people away, Wei He took down a complete record of the many methods Guan Ye taught for identifying poison in strange-beast meat. Guan Ye was worthy of her name as a poison-study professional; her research in this field went very deep, and she seemed to have her own systematic experience behind it.
Wei He saw the three of them off, and they agreed to meet again some other day in the provincial capital. That was where the Guan family's main house stood. After seeing them off, Wei He hurried back, and all of it was as if nothing had ever happened. Purely from the standpoint of profit, he should not have come. But Guan Ye had treated him with sincerity, true gold for a true heart — that great heap of things, worth tens of thousands.
And so Wei He had thought: he would come and take a quiet look; risking his life was out of the question, but he could lend a hand at the key moment, and count it as repaying Guan Ye's kindness. That much was still all right. So he had thought, and so he had done. And his good deed earned him no small return in the end.
Guan Ye gave him, without reserve, all her experience in judging poison in strange-beast meat. In this age such knowledge was, beyond any doubt, a first-rate means of making a living; if Wei He took it up and charged for it, he would be rich in no time.
