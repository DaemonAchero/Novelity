There was no other way a person could keep clothes at that degree of cleanliness at all hours. Wei He sighed inwardly: these people of old, with no washing machines, and still this standard of cleanliness — she had to be of no ordinary birth. And those soldiers… He turned his attention to the six soldiers guarding the woman.
Every one of these soldiers' leather cuirasses had the character Hong carved into it — not large, but remarkably clear and deep. Soon the woman, escorted by her line of soldiers, went down the stone stairs, off along the hill, and vanished into the dense woods.
"Tsk, tsk, tsk… Hong Family Fort's private soldiers — you can see at a glance they are not like ordinary men. Truly out of the common." A worshipper could not help praising them under his breath.
"Good rice and good meat served up to them every day, and I hear Hong Family Fort even teaches its private soldiers external-skill tempering within. With conditions like that, even a wastrel
could be piled up into a crack troop." Another stroked his beard and sighed.
"Then is that not simply an elite corps built up out of money? The garrison troops now are thin and weak beyond words, and here this Hong Family Fort secretly keeps private soldiers this strong. What they are after…" The worshippers still about at this hour were mostly no common folk, and now that they had fallen to chatting they all had their own experience and views.
They had seen at a glance that something was off about Hong Family Fort. Wei He said nothing. What Hong Family Fort did was no business of his; he had come only to find out where his parents had gone. He walked in through the gate, crossed the courtyard, and soon found a white-browed monk passing by. "Master."
The monk he had caught by the sleeve looked him up and down. "Reverence to you, benefactor."
"I would like to ask you something. Half a year ago, did this temple hire some stone carvers to come and carve Buddha images?" Wei He asked, low.

Down at the foot of Mingde Temple's hill. A tall, powerfully built man in a straw hat came slowly to the stone stairs going up. He took a dark green bamboo tube from his coat, shook it gently, brought it close to his ear, and listened carefully. From within the tube came faintly the sound of wings beating — three long, two short, in a most regular pattern.
The man twisted the lid of the tube; in the lid was a small black hole for air. He put his face to the hole and sniffed. "There's scent. One got missed here." These days he had been tracking black-letter worms without let-up, quietly silencing every living soul who had last come into contact with them.
What he relied on was the scent-seeking worm in this bamboo tube. The scent-seeking worm and the black-letter worm were born enemies, and the scent-seeker fed on black-letter worms alone; once it found them, or even people they had touched, it gave off a scent of itself. The scent was like sandalwood, soothing to the heart, and not to be forgotten; and the closer it came to the mark, the stronger the scent it gave.
The black-letter worm was harder to deal with still: once it had touched a person and left its dry trace, the scent would not disperse for months. By this method the man had already killed three of those the worms had touched, and disguised the corpses as the work of wild beasts. With his martial skill, that was not hard to bring off.
The man stepped out loose and easy and went up the stone stairs. His manner was unhurried, as if he were no different from the other worshippers come to bow to Buddha. He had grounds for that ease, and confidence in himself besides. Zhao Deli had been a thug, killed only because he was caught up in it as an innocent bystander; the sort of people he could have touched were ordinary folk,
nothing more. Even the slightly stronger ones were only a little more muscular low-ranking gang members. He was the hand sent to sweep this area clean, the one who did the silencing.
