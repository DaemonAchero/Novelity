"Did Chen Biao come by just now?" Wei He asked, low.
"He did. I kept quiet and didn't move, and h—he thought nobody was home, so he left." Wei Ying's words came out stumbling; she had clearly been badly frightened.
Wei He looked at the door and saw the fine yellow dust left on the wood where a boot had struck it. Whatever had happened outside, it had not been quiet.
"Big Sister took an important job. She won't be back for a while. Let's eat first." He kept his voice as steady as he could. It was only the two of them now; if he — the man of the house — panicked too, his timid sister would be terrified.
"Mm." Wei Ying answered in a small voice.
The two of them shut the door and closed the window, sat down in the near-darkness with only the sliver of light that came through the crack, and picked up their chopsticks.
The dishes were carrot fried with bean dregs, and a soup of cabbage and tofu. The rice was bran with a heavy hand of corn mixed through it. No meat; most families could not afford meat.
The reason Big Sister Wei Chun had grown so big and rough was her frame — and the extra rations the gang let her have, more than anyone at home ate. But the Wei family ate the same as everyone around them. A bit of grease in the pan counted as a good day.
After the meal, Wei Ying went to wash the bowls, and Wei He spread the assignments out on the only table and began working through them.
This was the only hour he dared strike a flint and light the oil lamp, writing by its light. Wei Ying sat beside him, mending clothes with careful stitches. Neither of them said much.
Night came, and each took their own bed.
The next day Wei He returned the finished work, and the money came through as well. At last he had saved enough to pay for fist lessons.
Big Sister Wei Chun still had not come back. And from his parents — Wei Tang and his wife — there was still no word.
Wei Ying no longer dared go out for work; she sat at home all day, waiting for Wei He to come and go and bring back some scrap of news.
Wei He went to the branch hall again. The man who had been willing to talk to him was nowhere to be seen, or perhaps simply had no more patience for him. He was turned back at the gate.
Three more days passed. No word of his parents. No word of Wei Chun.
Down at the mouth of their alley, Chen Biao and his lot were showing up more and more often, and standing there watching the Wei house for longer and longer stretches.
Wei He's heart had sunk to the bottom. He knew the worst of what he feared might already have happened. Whatever he was going to do, he had to do it fast.
In a world like this, people vanished all the time — robbers on the road, wild beasts, an illness on a journey that would not heal. There were far too many ways to disappear.
So on the morning of the fourth day, before first light, he took his money and went out.
Not a thread of dawn in the sky yet, Wei He took up a club as thick as his forearm and slipped quietly out of the house. He tightened the folds of the money he carried — every coin he had set aside to buy a master's teaching — and looked back at Second Sister Wei Ying.
"Stay hidden in the house. Don't go out. Don't make a sound."
"Mm." Wei Ying nodded hard. She seemed to have understood that something about her little brother was different today.
With Big Sister gone and their parents gone, it was only the two of them left to lean on each other, and she was determined not to be a weight on him.
By the dim, yellowing light before dawn, Wei He drew a deep breath and let it out slowly, calming himself.
"I meant to save a little more for food first. There's no time for that now…"
He could feel his heart pounding. What he carried was money he had scraped together over more than two months. Whether it worked or not came down to this one errand.
He hid the money pouch carefully in the inner layer of his clothes and tied it tight. Everything he had; not a coin of it could be lost. He covered the spot with his hand and the club, gripped the club tight, and walked quickly for the mouth of the alley.
He crossed Stone Bridge Street and passed over the stone bridge. Already there were farm folk on the road, carrying vegetables and fruit in to sell, and well-dressed men of means riding past in carriages, on their way out to enjoy the spring.
Farmers and poor folk in rough cloth, young gentlemen and young ladies in silk, sharing one road — the contrast could not have been sharper.
He had not gone far when he saw Chen Biao and his two men again, standing at the roadside shooting off their mouths.
