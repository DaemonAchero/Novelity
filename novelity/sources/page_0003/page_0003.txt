Bit by bit, it stored away.
At first Wei He had still hoped. Later — once three months had gone by — the thrill that hope brought had thinned into indifference.
He lay down on the bed in his clothes for a short rest. There were only three beds in the house: one for his parents, one his sisters squeezed into together, and one for him.
In a house of less than sixty square meters, three beds were crammed into one space, with the stove, the table, the chairs and everything else jostling around them. It was impossibly tight. Yet theirs still counted as a middle-income household among the commoners around them.
After dozing a while he got up, meaning to go out and walk.
He had the Realm Breaking Bead, and the thought was always there: if a chance came, find some decent martial arts and free-hand drills to practice, then use the bead to break through and rise.
But decent martial arts and free-hand drills were nowhere to be found. His eldest sister had carved out her place in the Black Water Gang only by being ruthless, by not caring whether she died, by striking hard and dirty — and even then she barely held her ground.
And the Realm Breaking Bead had not stirred in the slightest.
Helpless, Wei He made a habit of wandering a few rounds through the city after work each day. He was small, his clothes were worn, and one look told anyone he had no money, so no one came looking for trouble with him.
When he got up, Second Sister Wei Ying had already fallen asleep, slumped over the family's only table. Wei He slipped out quietly, eased the door shut, and walked out of the alley to the right of his home.
Cough, cough…
He had barely left the alley when bursts of coughing came from the right. He looked up and saw Sister Zhang — the neighbor who often went looking for work with Wei Ying — her hand clapped over her mouth, head down, coughing without pause.
"Xiao He, get back." Someone to one side pulled him away.
The one who pulled him was a short little old man with a long-stemmed pipe. He wore a sweat-stained white mandarin jacket that stank, and he drew Wei He past the mouth of the alley and a few steps beyond before he spoke, his voice low and his face grim.
"There's a plague loose in the city lately… I don't know what's wrong with that Zhang girl, but she's been coughing ever since she came back a few days ago. You mind yourself, don't catch it. And have your second sister mind herself too."
"Thank you, Grandpa Wang." Wei He's heart tightened.
He had heard about the plague some time ago; they said it had been spreading through the north of the city. And now it had come right to his own doorstep…
In these three months he had tried, too — tried to use the advanced thinking of a man who had crossed over to get his family out of poverty.
Every attempt had ended in failure.
Whatever method he tried, in this city everything always came back to one thing: the gangs.
The lower districts were carved into turf, each area run by a different gang.
A shop, a small trade — all of it had to pay them. With no backing and no patron, the moment your business picked up at all, eyes turned your way. And then it came down to one of two things: you handed the business over and became somebody's hired hand, or you paid out a heavy sum every month and kept only a little for your trouble.
This was a brutal age, one in which any commoner with a bit of money in his pocket became a target.
Wei He did not linger. He left the alley quickly, cut along one small path after another, and soon came to a somewhat old gray courtyard.
From inside came the steady sound of reciting aloud, broken now and then by an old man's low scolding.
Over the gate hung a plaque bearing three large red characters: Lecture Hall.
Wei He followed the wall, found a decent patch of ground, plopped down with his back to the wall, and listened in silence.
The Lecture Hall was where the city's wealthy families sent their children together for their first lessons. It was said that the instructors inside were all xiucai who had once passed the imperial examinations.
"I've about got the characters here down. Anywhere you go, letters and knowledge are the first thing you need to grasp anything at all."
He lowered his head and traced characters in the dust with his finger, practicing what he had learned.
"Wei He!"
A boy of about ten, his hair done up in two goat-horn braids, came sneaking up to his side.
