"Who's out there?!"
Chen Biao's voice came out of the yard, and there was puzzlement in it. Wei He pushed the gate open and went in.
In the middle of the yard, on the step in front of the inner room, sat Chen Biao: one arm bound in a grey cloth bandage with specks of blood soaking through it.
"Chen Biao. The moment I heard you were hurt I came straight here." Wei He smiled.
"You? Who are…?" Chen Biao got to his feet, puzzled. He had more to say, but all at once his eyes went blind — a fistful of white lime flung hard into his face.
Aah!!
Chen Biao flailed and struck out with his right hand, trying to beat off whatever was coming. Wei He drove a foot into his belly, picked up a hoe from the corner of the wall, and brought it down on Chen Biao's head, again and again, hard.
Bang! Bang! Bang! Bang!!
He threw the hoe aside, did not so much as look at the Chen Biao who lay soundless on the ground, turned and left the yard, and pulled the gate to behind him.
Then, without a word, he wrapped his head in cloth and walked quickly away. Only when he had left Nanshan Ward behind, reached the little river that runs between one ward and the next, and sat down in a patch of wild grass, did he let himself gasp — great heaving breaths, one after another. Everything had gone just as he had planned it from the first, and smoother than that besides. A wounded Chen Biao had no power of resistance left in him at all, and he had blinded the man with lime beforehand into the bargain.
He had rehearsed it so many times, and yet the doing of it had been far simpler than he imagined. That Chen Biao he had pictured to himself — a man of strength, a man whose presence cowed you — had not managed so much as a grunt under the whole of it. Finished, and that was the end of it.
Nobody had seen him come in or go out, he thought. His head had been wrapped the whole way, and the man had never called his name aloud. Only that widow Shan Ju had seen him, and she could not have seen his face. His hands shook as he unwound the grey cloth from his head and took off his outer coat, turning it so the specks of blood were hidden. What had become of Chen Biao? With that much force behind the blows, he ought to be…
He did not go on thinking it. In this world a man's death was a very ordinary thing; corpses were dragged out of the drains in the city often enough, and beyond the walls bones were gnawed by wild dogs, and the wild dogs were trapped and eaten in their turn. But this was different. This time Chen Biao might truly die, and die at his own hands. Wei He breathed deep, over and over, working at the state of his own body.
Chen Biao had never stopped eyeing Second Sister Wei Ying, and he made his living out of carrying people off; he had very nearly stolen the money Wei He needed for his training as well. If the man did not die, his second sister could never live at ease. So he was ridding the world of a pest. He was right. He was right. He kept finding reasons for himself, and all the while he knew better.
To begin with he had truly meant to cripple Chen Biao, not to kill him. But the moment it came to hands his mind went blank and all the strength in his body burst out at once; he was afraid Chen Biao would shout his name. To keep him from crying out he brought the hoe down on his head without thinking, and only after five or six blows did he come to himself and know his hand had been too heavy.
Now that he thought of it, his head had been wrapped, and the man facing him had probably had no idea who he was. And a wrought-iron hoe, brought down with the strength that let him lift an eighty-catty stone… and every blow aimed at the head… Wei He knew, in the part of him that was honest, that Chen Biao was finished. He had no fear of the government: the government no longer governed, and men died inside and outside the walls every day, and every day there was crime of every kind,
while the constables of the yamen were as though blind and deaf. They paid no heed at all. So this… this was what killing a man was like. Wei He's hand was still trembling, but he knew he had to get used to it. In an age like this, a man who was not ruthless could not live.
He did not want to die, so someone else would have to. A long while passed before he got to his feet, turned, and walked back into the ward.
