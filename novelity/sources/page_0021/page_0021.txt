Not long after. At the lodging of another of Chen Biao's followers, a small flat-roofed house. Wei He went in without any expression on his face, and came out again a while later with a little blood on his hands. The first time he had been a little afraid, but once he was used to it the tightness went out of him.
Then the third place: the lodging of Chen Biao's second follower, an earthen hut standing between two wards. By the time Wei He reached it, it was nearly dark. All round the hut was emptiness, a stretch of waste ground.
Outside the hut a great heap of dried corn stalks was stacked. Wei He went to the door and knocked softly. Thump, thump, thump. Silence. No sound from within.
Thump, thump, thump. He knocked again and waited. He waited a while; still not the least sound came from inside. After thinking it over, he went to the window and looked in through the crack.
Huts like this had doors and windows that never fitted tight; the gaps were wide, and come close and you could see the whole of what was inside. By the last of the daylight Wei He made out a man lying on his side on the bed within — a man in a short black mandarin jacket. Someone there.
He went back to the door and looked right and left. No one about. So he stepped back a few paces, rushed forward hard, and kicked. Bang! The broken wooden door of the hut flew open as easy as that.
He had trained the better part of a year, and his strength was no longer that of the thin, small man he had been. He was now built much as Chen Biao had been — the Chen Biao who used to put him on edge — and his strength was likely much the same. One or two men might not be enough to hold him down. The door had gone in, and still nothing stirred inside.
Wei He was puzzled, and wary, and stood in the doorway looking in. "Zhao Deli, get up," he said. The man on the bed did not move, as though he had not heard. In the hut hung a foul smell, like meat gone rotten.
Wei He's face changed a little; a guess began to form in him. He went nearer, reached out, and turned the man on the bed over. A face white as death, with nothing left on it but skin and bone, came before his eyes. The two eyeballs had shrivelled away and dried, and tiny black worms crawled in and out of the nostrils.
This Zhao Deli, Chen Biao's follower, had been dead no one knew how long, and no one had even found that he was dead. And the worst of it was the child nestled against his breast. The child lay curled into a ball, its small face all skin and bone as well, and out of its ear holes and its open little mouth there came the same
black worms, crawling in and out. Wei He's scalp crawled; he fell back a few steps and ran out of the hut, gasping in great mouthfuls of air. Part of it was fright at the corpse, but more of it was the reek. Three men: two dead by his own hand, and one who had been dead nobody knew how long.
The grudge was settled, and there was not one drop of gladness in him. He went to the river nearby, washed his hands, and walked back to the old house he lived in, wooden to the bone. In his head, over and over, turned the look of that last one — the dried corpse on the bed.
